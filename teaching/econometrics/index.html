01 / OVERVIEW

About this course

A graduate course in econometric theory and applied methods. The sequence develops probability and asymptotic foundations, linear estimation and inference, identification, cross-sectional and panel methods, time-series models, cointegration, and causal research design.

What you will learn

Students completing the course should be able to:

  1. derive OLS and IV estimators and characterize their statistical properties;
  2. distinguish identification from estimation and inference;
  3. select covariance estimators consistent with the data dependence structure;
  4. formulate and estimate panel and time-series models;
  5. evaluate cointegration, dynamic systems, and causal designs;
  6. conduct Monte Carlo experiments and produce reproducible empirical research.

How the course is organized

Each lecture session has a full set of written notes: learning objectives, formal development with derivations, worked examples, a problem set, a computational lab and readings. Problem sets with solution guidance and examination specifications are under Assignments and Exams.

Part I · Linear regression and inference Lectures 1–4

  1. From Probability to the Linear Regression Model
  2. OLS Inference: Sampling Distributions, Hypothesis Tests, and Confidence Intervals
  3. Functional Form, Partial Effects, and Omitted-Variable Bias
  4. Heteroskedasticity, Dependence, and Robust Inference

Part II · Endogeneity and identification Lectures 5–6

  1. Endogeneity, Instrumental Variables, and Two-Stage Least Squares
  2. Simultaneous Equations and Structural Identification

Part III · Panel data Lectures 7–8

  1. Panel Data: Fixed Effects, Random Effects, and Correlated Effects
  2. Dynamic Panels, Difference GMM, and System GMM

Part IV · Time-series econometrics Lectures 9–12

  1. Time-Series Foundations: Stationarity, ARMA, and Forecasting
  2. Unit Roots, Structural Breaks, and Persistence
  3. VAR and Structural VAR: Dynamic Interactions and Shock Identification
  4. Cointegration, VECM, and ARDL Models

Part V · Causal inference and research practice Lectures 13–14

  1. Causal Inference: Potential Outcomes, DiD, and Regression Discontinuity
  2. Research Design, Monte Carlo, Replication, and Reporting

Assessment

Problem sets 30%; replication project 25%; midterm 20%; final applied paper 25%. Graduate extension requires asymptotic derivations, identification defense, and Monte Carlo comparison.

Core references

  • Hayashi, Econometrics.
  • Wooldridge, Econometric Analysis of Cross Section and Panel Data.
  • Greene, Econometric Analysis.
  • Hamilton, Time Series Analysis.
  • Lütkepohl, New Introduction to Multiple Time Series Analysis.
  • Angrist and Pischke, Mostly Harmless Econometrics.
