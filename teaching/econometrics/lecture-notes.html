Lecture Notes

Theory, estimation, identification and applications — from projection geometry to causal research design.

Lec #Topic and objectivesLength
Part I · Linear regression and inference
1From Probability to the Linear Regression Model
By the end of this lecture you should be able to: 1. define the conditional expectation function (CEF) and prove that it is the best predictor of \(Y\) given \(X\) in mean square; 2. define the best linear predictor (linear projection), derive its coefficients, and explain how it relates to the CEF; 3. derive the OLS estimator in matrix form and interpret it as the sample analogue of the projection coefficient; 4. state and prove the algebraic properties of the projection matrices \(P_X\) and \(M_X\), including the Frisch–Waugh–Lovell theorem; 5. prove unbiasedness of OLS and the Gauss–Markov theorem, and state exactly which assumptions each result uses.
23 min read
2OLS Inference: Sampling Distributions, Hypothesis Tests, and Confidence Intervals
By the end of this lecture you should be able to: 1. state the convergence concepts and limit theorems used in econometrics (WLLN, CLT, continuous mapping, Slutsky, delta method); 2. prove consistency and asymptotic normality of OLS for the linear projection model; 3. derive the exact finite-sample distribution of \(t\) and \(F\) statistics in the normal regression model; 4. construct and interpret asymptotic \(t\) tests, Wald tests, and confidence intervals, including for nonlinear functions of \(\beta\); 5. explain size, power, and the duality between tests and confidence sets.
20 min read
3Functional Form, Partial Effects, and Omitted-Variable Bias
By the end of this lecture you should be able to: 1. interpret coefficients in level, log and mixed specifications exactly, not only approximately; 2. compute partial effects, turning points and their standard errors in models with polynomials and interactions; 3. derive the omitted-variable bias formula in finite samples and in the limit, and sign the bias; 4. derive attenuation bias from classical measurement error, and explain why "bad controls" bias regression coefficients; 5. use specification tests (RESET) and sensitivity analysis responsibly.
19 min read
4Heteroskedasticity, Dependence, and Robust Inference
By the end of this lecture you should be able to: 1. derive the heteroskedasticity-robust (HC) covariance estimators and explain the finite-sample corrections HC1–HC3; 2. test for heteroskedasticity and decide when weighted least squares is worth using; 3. derive the cluster-robust variance estimator and the Moulton factor, and explain what goes wrong with few clusters; 4. derive the long-run variance and the Newey–West HAC estimator for time-series regressions; 5. choose the level of clustering from the sampling and assignment design.
19 min read
Part II · Endogeneity and identification
5Endogeneity, Instrumental Variables, and Two-Stage Least Squares
By the end of this lecture you should be able to: 1. classify sources of endogeneity and show why each makes OLS inconsistent; 2. derive the IV and 2SLS estimators, prove consistency and asymptotic normality, and compute correct standard errors; 3. diagnose weak instruments and use weak-instrument-robust inference (Anderson–Rubin); 4. test overidentifying restrictions and endogeneity, and state what each test can and cannot establish; 5. interpret IV estimates with heterogeneous effects as local average treatment effects (LATE).
21 min read
6Simultaneous Equations and Structural Identification
By the end of this lecture you should be able to: 1. write a system of simultaneous equations in structural and reduced form, and derive simultaneity bias; 2. state and apply the order and rank conditions for identification of a single equation; 3. estimate an identified equation by 2SLS and LIML, and a full system by 3SLS, and compare their properties; 4. explain identification through covariance restrictions and heteroskedasticity; 5. connect the identification problem in simultaneous equations to structural VARs (Lecture 11).
18 min read
Part III · Panel data
7Panel Data: Fixed Effects, Random Effects, and Correlated Effects
By the end of this lecture you should be able to: 1. write the unobserved-effects model and explain what panel data can and cannot identify; 2. derive the fixed-effects (within) and first-difference estimators and state the strict-exogeneity assumption each needs; 3. derive the random-effects GLS estimator as quasi-demeaning and explain when it is consistent; 4. use the Mundlak (correlated random effects) device and a robust Hausman test to choose between FE and RE; 5. conduct inference with clustered standard errors, and recognize the limits of two-way fixed effects.
19 min read
8Dynamic Panels, Difference GMM, and System GMM
By the end of this lecture you should be able to: 1. derive the Nickell bias of the within estimator in a dynamic panel with short \(T\); 2. state the generalized method of moments (GMM) framework, the efficient weight matrix, and the \(J\) test; 3. derive the Anderson–Hsiao and Arellano–Bond (difference GMM) moment conditions; 4. derive the additional Blundell–Bond (system GMM) moment conditions and the initial-conditions assumption they require; 5. diagnose dynamic-panel GMM with AR(1)/AR(2) tests, Hansen tests, and instrument-count checks, and recognize instrument proliferation.
20 min read
Part IV · Time-series econometrics
9Time-Series Foundations: Stationarity, ARMA, and Forecasting
By the end of this lecture you should be able to: 1. define strict and weak stationarity, ergodicity and martingale differences, and explain why they replace i.i.d. sampling; 2. derive the moments, autocorrelation function and stationarity conditions of AR, MA and ARMA processes; 3. state the Wold decomposition and use ACF/PACF patterns and information criteria to choose a model; 4. estimate autoregressions by OLS and know when the usual asymptotics apply, including the inconsistency caused by a lagged dependent variable with serially correlated errors; 5. construct optimal forecasts, forecast intervals, and tests of relative forecast accuracy.
19 min read
10Unit Roots, Structural Breaks, and Persistence
By the end of this lecture you should be able to: 1. distinguish trend-stationary from difference-stationary (integrated) processes, and explain why the distinction matters for inference and policy; 2. derive the Dickey–Fuller regression, state its non-standard limiting distribution, and choose the correct deterministic terms and critical values; 3. use ADF, DF-GLS and KPSS tests together, and understand their size and power trade-offs; 4. test for structural breaks with known and unknown break dates (Chow, Andrews sup-\(F\), Bai–Perron); 5. explain why ignored breaks bias unit-root tests toward non-rejection, and how break-robust tests respond.
18 min read
11VAR and Structural VAR: Dynamic Interactions and Shock Identification
By the end of this lecture you should be able to: 1. specify and estimate a reduced-form VAR, check stability, choose lag length, and test Granger non-causality; 2. compute impulse responses and forecast-error variance decompositions from the VAR's moving-average representation; 3. state the structural identification problem precisely and count the restrictions needed; 4. implement and critique recursive (Cholesky), long-run, sign-restriction and external-instrument (proxy) identification; 5. compare VAR impulse responses with local projections, and construct valid confidence bands.
19 min read
12Cointegration, VECM, and ARDL Models
By the end of this lecture you should be able to: 1. define cointegration and explain why a regression in levels of \(I(1)\) variables can be meaningful; 2. state the Granger representation theorem and write a vector error-correction model (VECM); 3. implement the Engle–Granger and Johansen procedures, with the correct critical values and deterministic specification; 4. conduct valid inference on cointegrating vectors (DOLS, FMOLS, Johansen MLE) and test weak exogeneity; 5. use the ARDL bounds test, and explain when it is and is not the right tool.
20 min read
Part V · Causal inference and research practice
13Causal Inference: Potential Outcomes, DiD, and Regression Discontinuity
By the end of this lecture you should be able to: 1. define causal estimands (ATE, ATT, LATE, CATE) in the potential-outcomes framework, and decompose a naive comparison into effect and selection bias; 2. estimate effects under unconfoundedness by regression adjustment, inverse probability weighting and doubly robust methods, and check overlap; 3. set up difference-in-differences, test its assumptions with event studies, and explain why two-way fixed effects fails with staggered adoption and heterogeneous effects; 4. describe the synthetic control method and its inference; 5. estimate sharp and fuzzy regression discontinuity designs with local polynomials, optimal bandwidths and robust bias-corrected inference, and validate them.
22 min read
14Research Design, Monte Carlo, Replication, and Reporting
By the end of this lecture you should be able to: 1. move from a research question to a precisely stated estimand, an identification argument and an estimator; 2. design, run and report a Monte Carlo study, including Monte Carlo standard errors; 3. use the bootstrap correctly (pairs, wild, block, cluster), and know when it fails; 4. control for multiple testing (Holm, Benjamini–Hochberg, Romano–Wolf) and report specification robustness transparently; 5. build a replication package that meets current journal standards.
19 min read
