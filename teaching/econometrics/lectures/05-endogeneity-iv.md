# Lecture 05 — Endogeneity, Instrumental Variables, and Two-Stage Least Squares

## Learning objectives
By the end of this lecture you should be able to:

1. classify sources of endogeneity and show why each makes OLS inconsistent;
2. derive the IV and 2SLS estimators, prove consistency and asymptotic normality, and compute correct standard errors;
3. diagnose weak instruments and use weak-instrument-robust inference (Anderson–Rubin);
4. test overidentifying restrictions and endogeneity, and state what each test can and cannot establish;
5. interpret IV estimates with heterogeneous effects as local average treatment effects (LATE).

## Motivation
Economic data are generated by choices. People choose their schooling, firms choose their inputs, and governments choose policies in response to conditions. When a regressor is chosen with knowledge of factors that also affect the outcome, the regressor is correlated with the error and OLS no longer recovers the coefficient of interest. Instrumental variables offer a way out: find a source of variation in the regressor that is unrelated to the error. The method is powerful and widely misused. Its validity rests on an untestable exclusion restriction, its precision on instrument strength, and its interpretation on assumptions about who responds to the instrument.

## 1. Endogeneity
A regressor \(X_j\) in the **structural** equation \(Y=X'\beta+u\) is **endogenous** if \(E[X_ju]\neq0\). Here \(\beta\) is the causal or structural parameter, not the projection coefficient. OLS then converges to \(\beta+Q_{XX}^{-1}E[Xu]\neq\beta\). The classic sources are:

- **omitted variables** (Lecture 3): ability in a wage equation;
- **simultaneity** (Lecture 6): price and quantity determined jointly by supply and demand;
- **measurement error** (Lecture 3): observed schooling equals true schooling plus noise;
- **selection on gains**: those who expect larger returns choose more treatment.

## 2. The instrumental-variables estimator
Partition \(X=(X_1',X_2')'\) into exogenous regressors \(X_1\) (including the constant) and endogenous \(X_2\). An instrument vector \(Z=(X_1',Z_2')'\), with \(\ell\ge k\) elements, must satisfy:

<div class="definition" markdown="1">
**Instrument conditions.** (i) **Exogeneity:** \(E[Zu]=0\). (ii) **Relevance:** \(Q_{ZX}=E[ZX']\) has full column rank \(k\). The order condition \(\ell\ge k\) (at least as many excluded instruments as endogenous regressors) is necessary for (ii).
</div>

Exogeneity has two parts: the instrument is *as good as randomly assigned* with respect to \(u\), **and** it affects \(Y\) only through \(X\) (the **exclusion restriction**). With \(\ell=k\) (just identification), premultiplying \(Y=X'\beta+u\) by \(Z\) and taking expectations gives \(E[ZY]=E[ZX']\beta\), so
\[
\beta=E[ZX']^{-1}E[ZY],\qquad \widehat\beta_{IV}=(Z'X)^{-1}Z'y .
\]

**One regressor, one instrument.** \(\beta_1=\operatorname{Cov}(Z,Y)/\operatorname{Cov}(Z,X)\): the ratio of the **reduced form** to the **first stage**. With a binary instrument this is the **Wald estimator**
\[
\widehat\beta_{W}=\frac{\bar Y_{Z=1}-\bar Y_{Z=0}}{\bar X_{Z=1}-\bar X_{Z=0}} .
\]

## 3. Two-stage least squares
With \(\ell>k\) (over-identification), \(E[Z(Y-X'b)]=0\) is \(\ell\) equations in \(k\) unknowns. 2SLS uses the combination that projects \(X\) onto the instruments:
\[
\widehat\beta_{2SLS}=(X'P_ZX)^{-1}X'P_Zy,\qquad P_Z=Z(Z'Z)^{-1}Z' .
\]
**Two-stage interpretation.** Stage 1 regresses each column of \(X\) on \(Z\) to get \(\widehat X=P_ZX\). Stage 2 regresses \(y\) on \(\widehat X\). The point estimates coincide, because \(\widehat X'\widehat X=X'P_ZX\) and \(\widehat X'y=X'P_Zy\). **The second-stage OLS standard errors are wrong**, because they use residuals \(y-\widehat X\widehat\beta\) instead of \(y-X\widehat\beta\). Always use a dedicated IV routine.

**Never do "forbidden regressions".** Plugging in fitted values from a *nonlinear* first stage (probit, squared fitted values) does not give a consistent estimator in general. Linear projection of \(X\) onto \(Z\) is what makes the first-stage residuals orthogonal to everything in the second stage.

<div class="theorem" markdown="1">
**Theorem 5.1 (Asymptotics of 2SLS).** Under i.i.d. sampling, \(E[Zu]=0\), \(\operatorname{rank}(Q_{ZX})=k\), \(Q_{ZZ}\) positive definite, and finite fourth moments,
\[
\sqrt n(\widehat\beta_{2SLS}-\beta)\to_dN(0,V),\qquad V=(Q_{XZ}Q_{ZZ}^{-1}Q_{ZX})^{-1}Q_{XZ}Q_{ZZ}^{-1}\Omega Q_{ZZ}^{-1}Q_{ZX}(Q_{XZ}Q_{ZZ}^{-1}Q_{ZX})^{-1},
\]
with \(\Omega=E[ZZ'u^2]\). Under homoskedasticity, \(V=\sigma^2(Q_{XZ}Q_{ZZ}^{-1}Q_{ZX})^{-1}\).
</div>

<div class="proof" markdown="1">
*Proof.* \(\widehat\beta-\beta=\big[(\tfrac{X'Z}{n})(\tfrac{Z'Z}{n})^{-1}(\tfrac{Z'X}{n})\big]^{-1}(\tfrac{X'Z}{n})(\tfrac{Z'Z}{n})^{-1}\tfrac{Z'u}{n}\). Apply the WLLN to each moment matrix, the CLT to \(n^{-1/2}Z'u\to_dN(0,\Omega)\), and Slutsky. ∎
</div>

2SLS is the efficient GMM estimator under homoskedasticity. With heteroskedasticity, two-step GMM with weight matrix \(\widehat\Omega^{-1}\) is more efficient (Lecture 8).

**IV is less precise than OLS.** In the just-identified single-regressor case under homoskedasticity, \(\operatorname{avar}(\widehat\beta_{IV})=\operatorname{avar}(\widehat\beta_{OLS})/\rho^2_{XZ}\), where \(\rho_{XZ}\) is the correlation between \(X\) and \(Z\). With \(\rho_{XZ}=0.2\), the variance is 25 times larger. IV trades bias for variance.

## 4. Weak instruments
When the first stage is weak, the asymptotic normal approximation of Theorem 5.1 is poor even in large samples.

- **Bias toward OLS.** With many weak instruments, 2SLS is biased toward the OLS probability limit. Approximately, \(E[\widehat\beta_{2SLS}]-\beta\approx(\operatorname{plim}\widehat\beta_{OLS}-\beta)/(F+1)\), where \(F\) is the population first-stage \(F\) statistic (Staiger and Stock, 1997).
- **Size distortion.** Nominal 5% Wald tests can reject far more often than 5%, and confidence intervals undercover.
- **Bound et al. (1995)** showed that quarter-of-birth estimates of the return to schooling with many weak instruments could be reproduced with randomly generated instruments.

**Diagnosis.** With one endogenous regressor, report the first-stage \(F\) for the excluded instruments, computed with a variance estimator matching the error structure. Under heteroskedasticity or clustering, use the **effective \(F\)** of Montiel Olea and Pflueger (2013). The rule of thumb \(F>10\) (Stock and Yogo, 2005) limits the 2SLS bias to about 10% of the OLS bias. Lee, McCrary, Moreira and Porter (2022) show that a 5% \(t\)-test with \(|t|>1.96\) requires a first-stage \(F\) of about 104.7 for correct size, or else adjusted critical values (the **tF procedure**).

**Robust inference.** The **Anderson–Rubin (AR) test** of \(H_0:\beta=\beta_0\) regresses \(Y-X_2'\beta_0\) on \(Z\) and tests the coefficients on the excluded instruments \(Z_2\). Under \(H_0\) this is a valid test however weak the instruments are, because \(Y-X_2'\beta_0=X_1'\gamma+u\) has no endogenous regressor. Inverting the AR test over a grid of \(\beta_0\) yields a confidence set with correct coverage. The set may be unbounded when instruments are weak: that is an honest statement that the data are uninformative. In the just-identified case, AR is efficient among robust tests. With over-identification, the conditional likelihood ratio (CLR) test of Moreira (2003) has better power.

## 5. Testing
**Over-identification (Sargan–Hansen \(J\)).** With \(\ell>k\), test whether all moment conditions can hold simultaneously:
\[
J=n\,\bar g(\widehat\beta)'\widehat\Omega^{-1}\bar g(\widehat\beta)\to_d\chi^2_{\ell-k},\qquad \bar g(b)=n^{-1}Z'(y-Xb).
\]
Under homoskedasticity, \(J=nR^2\) from regressing the 2SLS residuals on \(Z\) (Sargan). **Limitations:** \(J\) tests coherence among instruments, not validity. If all instruments are invalid in the same direction, \(J\) has no power. And with heterogeneous effects, different valid instruments identify different LATEs, so \(J\) can reject even when all instruments are valid.

**Endogeneity (Durbin–Wu–Hausman).** Regress the endogenous regressor on \(Z\), save the first-stage residual \(\widehat v\), and add it to the structural equation (the **control function** regression). The OLS coefficients on \(X\) in this augmented regression equal 2SLS. A robust \(t\) test on \(\widehat v\) tests \(H_0:E[X_2u]=0\). Failing to reject is not evidence of exogeneity when the instruments are weak, because the test then has little power.

## 6. Heterogeneous effects and LATE
Let \(D\in\{0,1\}\) be a treatment with potential outcomes \(Y(1),Y(0)\), and \(Z\in\{0,1\}\) an instrument with potential treatments \(D(1),D(0)\). The population divides into **compliers** (\(D(1)=1,D(0)=0\)), **always-takers**, **never-takers** and **defiers**.

<div class="theorem" markdown="1">
**Theorem 5.2 (Imbens and Angrist, 1994).** Assume (i) independence: \((Y(1),Y(0),D(1),D(0))\perp Z\); (ii) exclusion: \(Z\) affects \(Y\) only through \(D\); (iii) first stage: \(P(D=1\mid Z=1)\neq P(D=1\mid Z=0)\); (iv) monotonicity: \(D(1)\ge D(0)\), so there are no defiers. Then
\[
\frac{E[Y\mid Z=1]-E[Y\mid Z=0]}{E[D\mid Z=1]-E[D\mid Z=0]}=E\big[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid D(1)>D(0)\big].
\]
</div>

<div class="proof" markdown="1">
*Proof.* \(Y=Y(0)+D\,(Y(1)-Y(0))\). By independence and exclusion, \(E[Y\mid Z=1]-E[Y\mid Z=0]=E[(D(1)-D(0))(Y(1)-Y(0))]\). Under monotonicity, \(D(1)-D(0)\in\{0,1\}\), so this equals \(P(\text{complier})\cdot E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid\text{complier}]\). The denominator equals \(P(\text{complier})\). ∎
</div>

The Wald/IV estimand is therefore the average effect **for compliers**, the people whose treatment the instrument changes. It is not, in general, the ATE, nor the effect on the treated. Different instruments move different people and identify different LATEs. External validity has to be argued, not assumed. The characteristics of compliers can be described from the data (Abadie, 2003), which helps that argument.

## Worked example
**Draft lottery and earnings (Angrist, 1990).** The instrument is draft eligibility \(Z\), assigned by lottery number; the treatment is Vietnam-era military service \(D\). Use stylized numbers. Among eligible men, 35% served; among ineligible men, 19% served. Mean annual earnings are \$15,300 for eligible and \$15,650 for ineligible men. Then
\[
\widehat\beta_W=\frac{15{,}300-15{,}650}{0.35-0.19}=\frac{-350}{0.16}=-2{,}188 .
\]
Interpretation: military service reduced annual earnings by about \$2,200 **for men who served because they were draft-eligible** (compliers). The lottery makes independence credible. The exclusion restriction could fail if eligibility changed behavior directly, for example through staying in school to obtain deferments. Always-takers (volunteers) and never-takers (the exempt) do not contribute to the estimate.

## Problem set
1. **IV with a binary instrument.** Show that \(\operatorname{Cov}(Z,Y)/\operatorname{Cov}(Z,X)\) equals the Wald estimator when \(Z\) is binary.

    <details class="solution" markdown="1"><summary>Solution</summary>

    For binary \(Z\) with \(p=P(Z=1)\), \(\operatorname{Cov}(Z,W)=p(1-p)\big(E[W\mid Z=1]-E[W\mid Z=0]\big)\) for any \(W\). Take the ratio for \(W=Y\) and \(W=X\); the factor \(p(1-p)\) cancels.
    </details>

2. **Measurement error and IV.** Two independent noisy measures of schooling are available: \(x_1=x^*+v_1\) and \(x_2=x^*+v_2\). Show that using \(x_2\) as an instrument for \(x_1\) consistently estimates \(\beta\) in \(y=\beta x^*+u\).

    <details class="solution" markdown="1"><summary>Solution</summary>

    \(y=\beta x_1+(u-\beta v_1)\). Then \(\operatorname{Cov}(x_2,u-\beta v_1)=0\) because \(v_1,v_2,u,x^*\) are mutually independent except through \(x^*\), and \(\operatorname{Cov}(x_2,x_1)=\operatorname{Var}(x^*)\neq0\). So \(\operatorname{plim}\widehat\beta_{IV}=\beta\operatorname{Var}(x^*)/\operatorname{Var}(x^*)=\beta\).
    </details>

3. **2SLS standard errors.** Explain precisely why manual two-step 2SLS yields incorrect standard errors, and write the correct homoskedastic variance estimator.

    <details class="solution" markdown="1"><summary>Solution</summary>

    The second step estimates \(\sigma^2\) from \(y-\widehat X\widehat\beta=u+(X-\widehat X)\beta\), which contains first-stage error. The correct estimator is \(\widehat\sigma^2(X'P_ZX)^{-1}\) with \(\widehat\sigma^2=n^{-1}\sum_i(Y_i-X_i'\widehat\beta)^2\), using the *original* \(X\).
    </details>

4. **Anderson–Rubin.** In a just-identified model with one endogenous regressor, show that the AR confidence set is unbounded whenever the first stage is not significant at the chosen level.

    <details class="solution" markdown="1"><summary>Solution</summary>

    The AR statistic for \(\beta_0\) is the Wald statistic for \(\pi_Y-\beta_0\pi_X=0\), where \(\pi_Y\) and \(\pi_X\) are the reduced-form and first-stage coefficients. As \(|\beta_0|\to\infty\), it converges to the Wald statistic for \(\pi_X=0\). If that is below the critical value, every sufficiently large \(|\beta_0|\) is accepted, so the set is unbounded.
    </details>

5. **LATE.** In the draft example, suppose 2% of men are defiers (they serve only if *not* eligible). Write the Wald estimand in terms of complier and defier effects, and explain why it need not lie between them.

    <details class="solution" markdown="1"><summary>Solution</summary>

    The numerator is \(\pi_c\Delta_c-\pi_d\Delta_d\) and the denominator \(\pi_c-\pi_d\), where \(\pi\) and \(\Delta\) are shares and average effects. The ratio is a weighted average with a **negative** weight on defiers, so it can lie outside \([\Delta_c,\Delta_d]\), even with the opposite sign when effects are heterogeneous.
    </details>

## Computational lab
**Goal:** see weak-instrument bias, size distortion, and the robustness of Anderson–Rubin.

```python
import numpy as np
rng = np.random.default_rng(5)

def draw(n=500, pi=0.05, rho=0.8, beta=1.0):
    z = rng.normal(size=n)
    e = rng.multivariate_normal([0, 0], [[1, rho], [rho, 1]], size=n)
    x = pi * z + e[:, 1]                       # first stage
    y = beta * x + e[:, 0]                     # structural equation, Corr(u, v) = rho
    b_ols = (x @ y) / (x @ x)
    b_iv = (z @ y) / (z @ x)
    u = y - b_iv * x
    se_iv = np.sqrt((z**2 * u**2).sum()) / abs(z @ x)  # robust just-identified IV s.e.
    wald_rej = abs((b_iv - beta) / se_iv) > 1.96
    r = y - beta * x                           # AR test of H0: beta = 1
    g = (z @ r) / (z @ z); er = r - g * z
    ar_rej = abs(g / (np.sqrt((z**2 * er**2).sum()) / (z @ z))) > 1.96
    F = ((z @ x)**2 / (z @ z)) / (((x - (z @ x) / (z @ z) * z)**2).sum() / (n - 1))
    return b_ols, b_iv, wald_rej, ar_rej, F

for pi in (0.05, 0.15, 0.5):
    R = np.array([draw(pi=pi) for _ in range(3000)])
    print(f"pi={pi}: median F={np.median(R[:,4]):6.1f}  median OLS={np.median(R[:,0]):.2f}  "
          f"median IV={np.median(R[:,1]):.2f}  Wald size={R[:,2].mean():.3f}  AR size={R[:,3].mean():.3f}")
```

Tasks: (i) explain why the median IV estimate moves toward the OLS limit as \(F\) falls; (ii) confirm that AR has correct size for every \(\pi\) while the Wald test does not; (iii) invert AR over a grid of \(\beta_0\) to build a confidence set. In R, `ivreg::ivreg()` or `fixest::feols(y ~ 1 | x ~ z)` with `fitstat(~ivwald)`; `ivmodel` for AR and CLR. In Stata, `ivreg2` (with `ffirst`) and `weakiv`.
