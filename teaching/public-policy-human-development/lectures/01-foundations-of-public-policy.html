LECTURE 01 / 15

Foundations of Public Policy

Level: Graduate | Field: Public Policy and Human Development

Public problems, collective action, policy cycles, instruments and the analytical role of evidence.

Learning objectives

  1. Distinguish public problems from private or organizational problems and explain when collective action is warranted.
  2. Map agenda setting, formulation, adoption, implementation and evaluation as an iterative policy process.
  3. Compare regulatory, fiscal, informational, organizational and direct-provision instruments.
  4. Construct a policy problem statement with a defined population, outcome, institutional setting and time horizon.

1. Conceptual foundations

Public policy is a purposive course of government action or deliberate inaction addressing a collectively recognized problem. A policy problem is not simply an undesirable statistic: it requires a normative benchmark, an affected population, a causal account and a feasible domain of public action. Problem definition is therefore consequential. Defining unemployment as an individual skills deficit leads to different instruments than defining it as weak labor demand, spatial mismatch or discrimination.

2. Analytical framework

Represent the policy process as a sequence of linked decisions: (i) recognition and agenda access; (ii) formulation of alternatives; (iii) authorization and financing; (iv) delivery and enforcement; and (v) monitoring, evaluation and revision. The sequence is not strictly linear. Implementation feedback can redefine the problem, while fiscal or political constraints can remove an instrument from the feasible set. A policy design can be represented as P=(G,T,I,B,A), where G is the goal, T the target population, I the instrument, B the budget and A the accountable delivery agency.

Formal representation and interpretation

A useful diagnostic is the results chain: resources → activities → outputs → outcomes → impacts. Each arrow embodies assumptions. If a cash-transfer programme has a budget and enrolment system (inputs), it may deliver transfers (outputs), but whether poverty falls (outcome) depends on transfer adequacy, take-up, prices and household responses.

3. Worked policy example

Suppose a district records a 20% school non-attendance rate among children aged 12–15. A weak problem statement says that education is poor. A policy-ready statement specifies the district, age group, baseline attendance measure, comparison period and plausible constraints. Household survey interviews may reveal transport costs, child work and school quality as separate mechanisms. A transport subsidy, income support and school-quality intervention therefore become competing instruments rather than interchangeable slogans.

4. Seminar discussion

  1. What evidence distinguishes a market failure from an equity concern in a given policy problem?
  2. At which policy-cycle stage can administrative capacity most plausibly alter the original design?
  3. How would the problem definition change if the outcome were learning rather than enrolment?

Problem set

  1. Write a 150-word problem statement for a measurable human-development problem, specifying population, geography, baseline and time horizon.
  2. Construct a results chain for one policy intervention and identify one assumption at each transition.
  3. Compare a tax incentive, a regulation and direct public provision against the same policy objective.

Applied policy lab

Prepare a two-page policy concept note using a national or subnational case. Include a problem tree, stakeholder map, at least three indicators, two feasible instruments, an indicative budget category and a monitoring plan. Separate observed facts from causal hypotheses.

Required output: State the research question, population, geography, period, indicator definitions, data sources and assumptions. Distinguish descriptive findings from causal claims and document limitations.

Further reading

Dye, Understanding Public Policy; Howlett, Ramesh and Perl, Studying Public Policy; Kingdon, Agendas, Alternatives, and Public Policies.
