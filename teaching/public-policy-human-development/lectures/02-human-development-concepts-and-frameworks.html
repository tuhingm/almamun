LECTURE 02 / 15

Human Development: Concepts and Frameworks

Level: Graduate | Field: Public Policy and Human Development

Capabilities, functionings, agency, freedom, resources and the multidimensional meaning of development.

Learning objectives

  1. Explain the distinction between resources, capabilities, functionings and achieved outcomes.
  2. Use the capability approach to assess individual and group opportunity sets.
  3. Distinguish income growth from broader changes in well-being and agency.
  4. Apply a human-development framework to a concrete policy choice.

1. Conceptual foundations

Human development concerns the expansion of substantive freedoms and the opportunities people have to lead lives they value. The capability approach distinguishes resources (income, services, assets) from capabilities (real opportunities) and functionings (the beings and doings actually achieved). Equal resources do not necessarily generate equal opportunities because conversion factors differ across people and places: disability, gender norms, transport, safety, local service quality and household bargaining can all affect what a person can do with the same resource bundle.

2. Analytical framework

Let x_i denote the resource bundle of person i and z_i the personal, social and environmental conversion factors. The feasible capability set can be written C_i=F(x_i,z_i), where F maps resources and conversion factors into attainable functionings. Observed achievement y_i is one element selected from C_i. Policy can therefore expand development by increasing resources, improving conversion factors, widening choice or protecting agency. A rise in observed achievement alone does not reveal whether the opportunity set expanded.

Formal representation and interpretation

A policy evaluation framed only around average income may miss non-income constraints. A capability-oriented assessment can report a vector y_i=(health, education, material security, participation, safety) and examine both its levels and the distribution of feasible opportunities.

3. Worked policy example

Two rural households receive the same education stipend. One has a nearby secondary school and safe transport; the other faces a long journey, unpaid care demands and safety concerns. The transfer is identical, but its conversion into attendance differs. A capability diagnosis therefore considers transport, school location and care constraints alongside transfer size.

4. Seminar discussion

  1. Why are capabilities conceptually different from achieved functionings?
  2. Which conversion factors matter most in a rural education intervention?
  3. Can a policy improve an average functioning while reducing agency for a subgroup?

Problem set

  1. Construct a resource–conversion factor–capability–functioning diagram for a health or education programme.
  2. Identify four non-income dimensions that should accompany household income in a development assessment.
  3. Explain how two individuals with equal income may have different capability sets.

Applied policy lab

Select one development programme and create a capability matrix for at least three population groups. Identify the resource supplied, relevant conversion factors, intended functioning, possible agency effects and indicators needed to observe each dimension.

Required output: State the research question, population, geography, period, indicator definitions, data sources and assumptions. Distinguish descriptive findings from causal claims and document limitations.

Further reading

Sen, Development as Freedom; Sen, Inequality Reexamined; Nussbaum, Creating Capabilities; UNDP, Human Development Report.
