LECTURE 12 / 15

Sustainable Development and Integrated Policy Design

Level: Graduate | Field: Public Policy and Human Development

SDGs, systems interactions, policy coherence, trade-offs, integrated planning and multi-criteria appraisal.

Learning objectives

  1. Define the central concepts and distinguish related measures.
  2. Explain the analytical framework, assumptions and causal mechanisms.
  3. Apply the framework to a policy problem using appropriate evidence.
  4. Interpret distributional implications and communicate limitations.

1. Conceptual foundations

Sustainable development links present well-being to the ability of future generations to meet their needs. Economic, social and environmental outcomes interact: energy policy affects emissions, employment, household budgets and health; agricultural policy affects food security, water, land and rural incomes. Sector-by-sector planning can miss spillovers and contradictory incentives. Policy coherence asks whether goals, instruments, budgets and institutional mandates reinforce or undermine one another across sectors and time.

2. Analytical framework

Represent a policy system as a directed network: nodes are outcomes or instruments and edges represent causal or institutional links. A theory of change specifies how activities generate outputs and outcomes; a systems map highlights feedback loops, delays and unintended consequences. Integrated appraisal identifies affected groups, geographic scale, time horizon, externalities, distributional incidence and uncertainty. Multi-criteria analysis makes trade-offs explicit but cannot eliminate normative choices.

3. Worked policy example

A coal-region transition plan may reduce emissions while lowering local employment and municipal revenue in the short run. An integrated package combines clean-energy investment, worker retraining, income support, local diversification and fiscal transition assistance. Track emissions, job quality, household income, energy affordability and local public revenue together; a single environmental indicator cannot describe the whole transition.

4. Seminar discussion

  1. Which outcome dimensions are hidden by the headline indicator?
  2. Which groups face different constraints or treatment?
  3. What evidence would distinguish competing explanations?
  4. What assumptions are required for the proposed analysis?

Problem set

  1. Construct a systems map linking energy, employment, health and household welfare.
  2. Prepare a theory of change for integrated rural development.
  3. Build a multi-criteria matrix and test how priorities change under alternative weights.
  4. Explain why a weighted score is not a value-free ranking.

Applied policy lab

Develop an integrated policy framework for a selected SDG challenge. Include a systems map, policy-coherence matrix, distributional assessment, financing channels, institutional responsibilities and a monitoring dashboard with leading and lagging indicators.

Required output: State the population, geography, period, indicator definitions, data sources and assumptions. Separate descriptive findings from causal claims.

Further reading

United Nations, Transforming our World: the 2030 Agenda; OECD, Policy Coherence for Sustainable Development guidance; Meadows, Thinking in Systems.
