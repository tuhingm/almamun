LECTURE 13 / 15

Climate Change, Energy Transition and Social Development

Level: Graduate | Field: Public Policy and Human Development

Energy access, affordability, labor-market adjustment, climate vulnerability, distributional incidence and just-transition policy.

Learning objectives

  1. Define the central concepts and distinguish related measures.
  2. Explain the analytical framework, assumptions and causal mechanisms.
  3. Apply the framework to a policy problem using appropriate evidence.
  4. Interpret distributional implications and communicate limitations.

1. Conceptual foundations

The energy transition changes the technologies, prices, infrastructure and institutions through which energy is produced and consumed. Its social effects operate through household energy expenditure and reliability; direct and indirect employment; local fiscal revenues; health effects from pollution; land and resource use; and exposure to climate hazards. Costs and benefits depend on household energy budgets, worker skills, regional industrial structure, ownership and access to finance. Average national gains can coexist with concentrated local losses.

2. Analytical framework

Energy poverty is multidimensional: access, affordability, reliability, clean cooking, thermal comfort and the ability to use energy for essential services all matter. A first-order approximation to the real-income effect of a price change is ΔW_h≈−s_h^e Δp_e, where s_h^e is the baseline energy budget share and Δp_e is the proportional price change. This omits substitution, transfers, wage changes and health co-benefits. A just-transition dashboard should track emissions, employment, real disposable income, energy burden, health exposure and regional fiscal capacity, disaggregated by worker, household and place.

3. Worked policy example

Suppose an energy-price reform raises household energy costs by 8% before compensation. A household spending 12% of its budget on energy faces an approximate burden of 0.96% of total expenditure; a household spending 4% faces 0.32%. The reform may be regressive before revenue recycling. Compare targeted transfers, lifeline tariffs and efficiency retrofits, including fiscal cost, targeting error and incentive effects.

4. Seminar discussion

  1. Why can energy expenditure shares understate energy deprivation?
  2. Which workers and regions are exposed to transition risk?
  3. How can revenue recycling protect low-income households while preserving decarbonization incentives?
  4. Construct a transition exposure matrix by sector, occupation, region and skill level?

Problem set

  1. Define the policy problem, target population and principal outcome.
  2. Specify the assumptions and evidence required to support the proposed analysis.
  3. Develop a distributional or sensitivity analysis and explain its implications.
  4. Identify two implementation risks and propose measurable mitigation strategies.

Applied policy lab

Prepare a just-transition assessment for an energy-intensive region. Combine energy affordability indicators, employment structure, local fiscal dependence and climate exposure. Propose a sequenced package for workers, households and local government, with baseline values and measurable indicators.

Required output: State the population, geography, period, indicator definitions, data sources and assumptions. Separate descriptive findings from causal claims.

Further reading

ILO, Guidelines for a Just Transition towards Environmentally Sustainable Economies and Societies; IPCC, AR6 Synthesis Report; IEA, World Energy Outlook; World Bank climate and development reports.
