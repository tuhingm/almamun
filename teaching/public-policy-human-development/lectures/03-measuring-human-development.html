LECTURE 03 / 15

Measuring Human Development

Level: Graduate | Field: Public Policy and Human Development

Composite indicators, normalization, weighting, aggregation, uncertainty and distribution-sensitive measurement.

Learning objectives

  1. Explain the construction and interpretation of composite human-development indicators.
  2. Calculate normalized dimension indices and geometric aggregation.
  3. Assess sensitivity to weights, thresholds, missing data and measurement error.
  4. Distinguish national averages from within-country distribution.

1. Conceptual foundations

A composite index compresses multiple dimensions into a summary statistic. The compression improves communication but introduces choices about dimensions, indicators, normalization, weights and aggregation. A dimension indicator must have a clear construct, population coverage, direction of desirability, reference period and documented source. Comparability requires consistent definitions across places and time; a change in survey instrument or administrative reporting can create an apparent trend without a real outcome change.

2. Analytical framework

For a positive indicator x with lower and upper goalposts L and U, a min–max normalized index is I=(x−L)/(U−L). For a negative indicator, reverse the numerator: I=(U−x)/(U−L). A weighted arithmetic index H_A=Σ_j w_j I_j permits compensation across dimensions. A geometric index H_G=Π_j I_j^{w_j}, with Σw_j=1, penalizes imbalance more strongly. Neither aggregation rule is value-free: the choice determines how much a strong dimension can offset a weak one.

Formal representation and interpretation

For three dimension indices I_H, I_E and I_Y with equal weights, H=(I_H I_E I_Y)^(1/3). If the indices are 0.80, 0.60 and 0.40, H=(0.192)^(1/3)≈0.577. The arithmetic mean is 0.600. The geometric mean is lower because it penalizes the uneven dimension profile.

3. Worked policy example

Consider a district with health, education and income indices of 0.80, 0.60 and 0.40. The geometric index is approximately 0.577. If education improves to 0.70, the index becomes (0.80×0.70×0.40)^(1/3)≈0.607. Report the component indices as well as the aggregate: the summary alone hides which dimension changed.

4. Seminar discussion

  1. How do goalpost choices affect comparisons across years?
  2. When is geometric aggregation substantively preferable to arithmetic aggregation?
  3. What information is lost when an index is reported only as a national average?

Problem set

  1. Normalize a positive and a negative indicator using stated goalposts.
  2. Calculate arithmetic and geometric indices for the example and interpret the difference.
  3. Design a sensitivity table using alternative weights and one alternative normalization rule.

Applied policy lab

Using publicly available country or subnational indicators, construct a three-dimension index. Document source, units, years, missing-data treatment, goalposts and weights. Recalculate the ranking under equal weights and one alternative weighting scheme; report rank changes and component scores.

Required output: State the research question, population, geography, period, indicator definitions, data sources and assumptions. Distinguish descriptive findings from causal claims and document limitations.

Further reading

UNDP, Human Development Report: Technical Notes; Alkire and Foster (2011), Journal of Public Economics; OECD/JRC, Handbook on Constructing Composite Indicators.
